Bayesian Epistemic Uncertainty for Reasoning Trajectory
Selection in Language Model Fine-Tuning
Introduction and Motivation
Large language models demonstrate significantly improved performance in mathematical and
multi-step reasoning when trained to generate intermediate reasoning steps rather than
directly predicting the final answer (Wei et al., 2022). This establishes the foundation of
many works that use reasoning trajectory as training data, including bootstrap rationales for
hard problems (Zelikman et al., 2022) and methods that use a verifier to select the one ranked
highest among many generated candidate solutions. In this method, the quality of selected
reasoning trajectories is crucial. If a stronger teacher model can generate several different
step-by-step reasoning solutions to each math problem, a student model may learn more math
knowledge and concepts compared to others. Utilizing the teacher model’s reasoning dataset
is essential due to benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH manifests that mathematical
reasoning remains challenging even for large modern transformer models (Cobbe et al., 2021;
Hendrycks et al., 2021).
The core idea is to prefer training data that the student model finds uncertain, because those
samples may contain information that the student model has not yet learned. Nonetheless,
uncertainty is the clue that we can use to identify which datasets have the most information
that the model lacks. Kendall and Gal (2017) distinguish uncertainty into two types. Aleatoric
uncertainty, which comes from ambiguity or variability in data, and epistemic uncertainty,
which reflects a lack of knowledge in the model itself. For reasoning trajectories, this
difference is significant. A student model may be assigned high uncertainty (entropy) from
the generated solution by the teacher model since the underlying mathematical concept is
unfamiliar, but it may also do the same thing due to the text generation using unusual
wording, formatting, or an uncommon reasoning path. If our data trajectory is selected based
on total predicted entropy, then the data selection method may overemphasize noisy or
semantic variable examples rather than the examples that focus on the math knowledge that
better improve the student model’s reasoning steps.
This project studies whether a modern Bayesian theory of uncertainty can improve reasoning-
data selection. In the experiments, I compare two trajectory-selection methods for teacher-
generated math solutions. The baseline rule selects the trajectory with the highest total
predictive entropy, while the proposal method uses the rule that selects the trajectory with the
highest epistemic uncertainty. To calculate the epistemic uncertainty, we implement the
technique of Bayesian approximation by mutual information under Monte Carlo dropout (Gal
and Ghahramani, 2016). In the experiment, a large teacher model generates multiple
candidate trajectories for each math question. A Qwen2.5-3B student model then scores these
trajectories and is subsequently fine-tuned via LoRA on the datasets produced by the two
selection rules. The trained student models are then evaluated on GSM8K and MATH-500.
The study further analyzes whether separating epistemic uncertainty from total uncertainty
provides a more informative fine-tune dataset and leads to better mathematical reasoning
performance.
Method
In this project, I use a teacher-student pipeline to compare two uncertainty-based rules for

reasoning trajectory selection. First, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct serves as the teacher model and
generates five candidate step-by-step solutions for each math training problem. The multiply
generated trajectories provide distinct possible reasoning for the same question and let the
student model determine which sample may be most informative for learning.
To score these trajectories, Qwen2.5-3B serves as the student model. Instead of treating it as a
traditional fully deterministic model, I approximate Bayesian uncertainty using Monte Carlo
dropout. During inference time, dropout remains active for scoring, but gradients are disabled
to avoid weight updates.
For each candidate trajectory, the model performs 10 stochastic forward passes under teacher
forcing, meaning that the model evaluates the fixed reasoning trace token by token. The
repeated passes generate slightly different token probability distributions, which are used for
estimating data information uncertainty for the student model.
From these outputs, I compute two token-level uncertainty measures. The first is total
predictive entropy, which captures the overall uncertainty of the average predictive
distribution. The second is mutual information (MI), computed as total entropy minus
expected entropy across the 10 stochastic passes. In this project, total entropy is regarded as
the baseline uncertainty, while MI is used as an approximation of epistemic uncertainty, since
it represents disagreement across stochastic passes and better captures the knowledge the
student model lacks rather than surface-level variability.
In order to cover the token-level uncertainty into a single trajectory score, I compute and rank
the uncertainty values for each trajectory and average the top 50 tokens. This top-50
aggregation focuses on the most informative parts of the reasoning chain rather than diluting
the score across many low-information tokens within a single generated solution. Each
candidate trajectory receives both a total entropy score and an MI score.
These scores allow building separate fine-tuning datasets. In the baseline method, it selects
the trajectory with the highest total entropy for each question. In the proposal method, it
selects the trajectory with the highest MI. This produces two training datasets with different
selection rules. These training sets are then used to fine-tune separate LoRA adapters on the
Qwen2.5-3B base model and evaluate the trained models on GSM8K and MATH-500. This
experiment and setup control the effect of the trajectory selection and compare the total
predictive entropy and epistemic uncertainty for reasoning-data curation.
Experimental Setup
The training pipeline began with 12,000 unique math problems. For each problem, the
teacher model, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, generated five candidate reasoning trajectories,
producing 60,000 total candidate solutions. Each candidate was then scored by a Qwen2.5-3B
student model with Monte Carlo dropout implementation. For each math problem, I selected
one trajectory with the highest total predictive entropy to consist of the baseline dataset and
one trajectory with the highest mutual information (MI) to consist of the epistemic dataset.
Each of the two final fine-tune datasets contains 12,000 samples with only a difference in the
trajectory-selection rule.
To compare the total predictive entropy and MI of datasets, I fine-tuned two LoRA adapters
on the same base student model, Qwen2.5-3B, using identical hyperparameters. The LoRA
configuration used rank 16, LoRA alpha 32, and dropout. Training was conducted using 2
epochs with a per-device batch size of 4, gradient accumulation of 4, learning rate  2 × 10−4,
and maximum sequence length of 4096.

After the training procedure, both fine-tune models were evaluated on two standard
mathematical reasoning benchmarks: MATH-500 and GSM8K. During the evaluation
process, the models generated a single response for each math question. I then evaluate the
models by extracting the first boxed answer from the generation and mark the prediction for
the question correctly if the extracted string exactly matches the ground truth. The ACC@1
metric was used to compute and represent the model's accuracy on both benchmarks. The
reference answers for GSM8K and MATH-500 were numeric solutions and open-ended
answers, respectively. One limitation of the benchmark method is that using exact string
matching will penalize formatting differences even when the underlying answer is
mathematically correct.
Results
Table 1 shows the experiment results. The model trained on the MI dataset slightly
outperformed the model trained on the total-entropy dataset in both benchmarks. On MATH-
500, accuracy increased from 46.20% to 46.40%. On GSM8K, the accuracy increased from
79.30% to 79.45%. Although these gains are small, they are consistent across both evaluation
results.

The most significant interpretation is that the two selection rules produced very different
training datasets while resulting in only small downstream differences. Across the 12,000
training problems, the total-entropy and MI rules selected different trajectories for 8,105
problems, or 67.54% of the dataset. In other words, the uncertainty metric change, which
examples were originally chosen for fine-tuning on the baseline model. Despite this large
dataset-level difference, the final benchmark gap was very small. This concludes that many
alternative teacher trajectories may be similarly useful for supervised fine-tuning, or that the
current evaluation may be too limited to show meaningful differences in reasoning quality.
Conclusion
In this study, the results provide modest evidence for using mutual information as a data
trajectory selection method. The MI-based model performed slightly better than the total-
entropy model on both MATH-500 and GSM8K, which suggests that epistemic uncertainty
may help select reasoning data. However, the gains were very small, so the results are not

strong enough to claim that MI is clearly better than total predictive entropy.
In conclusion, Bayesian epistemic uncertainty is a promising technique for reasoning
trajectory selection, but in this project, it produced only modest improvements. The results
suggest that MI-based selection is worth further research with stronger evaluation methods,
multiple training runs, and broader experiments.
